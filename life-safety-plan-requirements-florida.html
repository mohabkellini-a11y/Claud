What is a life safety plan and when is it required in Florida?

A life safety plan is a drawing sheet — or set of sheets — showing the code-required life safety features of a building: exits and egress paths, exit signage, fire-rated separations, occupancy classifications, and occupant loads. Florida AHJs typically require life safety plans as part of the permit drawing set for commercial projects, and missing or incorrect life safety information is one of the most common reasons commercial submittals stall in plan review.

The Document

What a life safety plan shows

A life safety plan consolidates everything a plan reviewer needs to verify means of egress and passive fire protection into one coordinated set of sheets. Rather than hunting through architectural, structural, and MEP drawings for code compliance information, the reviewer checks the life safety plans first — which is why their accuracy drives the pace of the entire permit review.

  • Exit locations, egress paths, and exit discharge points
  • Occupant loads calculated for each room and floor
  • Occupancy classifications and occupancy separations
  • Fire-resistance ratings of walls, floors, corridors, and opening protectives
  • Travel distances, dead-end corridor lengths, and common path of travel
  • Exit signage, emergency lighting, and areas of refuge where required
When It Applies

When Florida AHJs require life safety plans

  • New commercial, industrial, institutional, and multi-family construction
  • Additions and renovations that alter egress paths, exits, or occupancy
  • Change of occupancy or change of use
  • Tenant improvements in assembly, educational, and high-hazard occupancies

Exact requirements vary by jurisdiction — some AHJs publish a life safety plan checklist, others evaluate it as part of the architectural review. Confirm expectations with the local AHJ before the drawing set goes out, because discovering the requirement at intake costs weeks.

Responsibility

Who prepares the life safety plan

The architect of record typically produces the life safety plan sheets as part of the construction documents. But the sheets are only as good as the analysis behind them — and that is engineering work. A fire protection engineer provides the occupant load calculations, verifies egress capacity and travel distances against the code, determines required fire-resistance ratings, and reviews the sheets for the deficiencies that trigger review comments. EverSafe Fire Protection supports architects and MEP firms with life safety analysis, plan review of life safety sheets, and PE-stamped documentation.

Avoid Delays

Deficiencies that stall plan review

  • Missing or miscalculated occupant loads
  • Egress paths that exceed allowable travel distances
  • Dead-end corridors beyond what the code permits
  • Exit doors and openings shown without the required fire ratings
  • Occupancy separations drawn on plan but not detailed as rated assemblies
  • Life safety sheets that disagree with the architectural floor plans

Every one of these is correctable — but each correction means a resubmittal cycle. An engineering review of the life safety sheets before submittal catches them when they are still cheap to fix.
